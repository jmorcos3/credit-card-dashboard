The majority ladder What each seat count actually unlocks, with the market-implied probability of reaching it.

Probabilities are read off Polymarket's Republican-seat-count brackets, so they will not match the control market exactly. Senate control for Republicans requires 50 seats, not 51, because Vice President Vance breaks ties. Democrats therefore need 51 (net +4 from 47).

435 seats; 218 is a majority of the full House. With vacancies the working threshold drops to a majority of seated members. Governing comfort matters more than the headline: a 3-seat majority loses votes to a single absence or defection.

Senate. 50 + VP: whichever party has 50 controls if it also holds the vice presidency, so the GOP's floor for control is 50. 51: a true majority, which sets the agenda, chairs every committee, confirms judges and cabinet (all now simple-majority after the nuclear option), and passes budget reconciliation. 60: cloture, the only way to move ordinary legislation past a filibuster; neither party is anywhere near it. 67: veto override, impeachment conviction, treaty ratification, constitutional amendments.

House. 218: majority of 435, elects the Speaker and controls the floor and every committee. There is no filibuster, so a bare majority passes anything on a party-line vote, but with no slack. 290: two-thirds for veto overrides. Discharge petitions also need 218 signatures, which is how a narrow majority gets bypassed on individual bills.

Trifecta vs. divided government. Republicans currently hold the White House, Senate and House. A Democratic House alone ends the trifecta: it blocks legislation and gains subpoena power. A Democratic Senate adds control of confirmations. Neither chamber can override a veto without two-thirds, so a Democratic sweep means gridlock rather than a Democratic agenda.
